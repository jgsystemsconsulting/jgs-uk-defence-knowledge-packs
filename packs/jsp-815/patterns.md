# Patterns: jsp-815

## Plan-Do-Check-Act cycle
When: building or reviewing Defence Area safety management arrangements under JSP 815 (ch02).
How: organise work on the four stages - Plan (policy, governance, planning), Do (implement arrangements and controls), Check (measure performance and investigate failures), Act (review and improve) - and close the loop as continual improvement; map the 12 Elements across the cycle (ch02).
Trade-offs: PDCA is the shape of the SMS, not a blank template that replaces Element Expectations or Area-specific governance (ch02).

## Three Lines of Defence assurance
When: designing who delivers assurance and how lines hand off (ch02, ch10).
How: 1LOD self-assurance by those delivering the activity; 2LOD function oversight (including DDSS roles the Framework describes); 3LOD independent review (including DSA and other independent review) against SofS Policy Statement and pan-Defence safety policy (ch10).
Trade-offs: independent review supports Area improvement and departmental reporting; it does not remove 1LOD ownership of control effectiveness (ch10).

## ALARP and tolerability
When: deciding whether residual safety risk may be accepted under Defence risk management (ch05, ch06).
How: reduce risk as low as reasonably practicable; judge tolerability separately; elevate when residual risk exceeds the authority of the commander or manager, including reasonably foreseeable risk to life (ch05); those controlling activity may pause or cease work when the standard fails (ch06).
Trade-offs: ALARP alone is not the whole test; local acceptance beyond delegated authority without elevation fails Expectation 4.3 (ch05).

## Safe System of Work
When: every Defence activity needing control measures from risk assessment (ch06).
How: group controls as safe person, safe practice, safe place, and safe equipment; apply to military and civilian employees and people working on behalf of Defence, in any location; follow named JSP 375 Chapter 8 method detail outside this pack (ch06).
Trade-offs: SSW is the control envelope, not a substitute for Duty Holding appointments, letters of delegation, or contractor 4Cs where those duties apply (ch06).

## Exemption certificate process
When: statute cannot be met and a managed exemption path is required under Element 3 / Annex B (ch04).
How: exhaust compliance options first; run the Annex B certificate steps with residual ALARP controls; keep the certificate under review when assumptions break or it expires (ch04).
Trade-offs: seeking exemption before exhausting compliance, or running on an expired certificate, is an anti-pattern (ch04).

## Safety case when complexity demands it
When: complex systems or activities need a maintained demonstration that risk is ALARP and tolerable beyond a single risk assessment (ch05; equipment lifecycle ch08).
How: develop, maintain, and review the safety case where required; track change across equipment, operations, infrastructure, training, people, plans, and procedures (ch05); for equipment, build and use the case across CADMID/T (ch08).
Trade-offs: writing a safety case for routine low-complexity work already covered by risk assessment and SOPs wastes effort and confuses Expectation 4.7 (ch05).

## Leading, lagging, and cultural indicators
When: setting safety performance monitoring under Element 9 (ch09).
How: use documented leading (predictive / process health), lagging (realised outcomes), and cultural (behaviours and reporting climate) indicators together; review trends, produce management information in time for decisions, and assess safety impact in cost/schedule/capability choices (ch09).
Trade-offs: injury counts alone are not a complete picture; MI that arrives after forums lock cost and schedule fails the decision link (ch09).

## Annex H audit cycle
When: planning or running 1LOD audit (and reusable discipline for wider programmes) under Annex H V1.2 (ch10).
How: own purpose; set or join the programme; plan and notify; pre-audit; research and document review; gather evidence; open; evaluate and rate; site-verify; close; report; debrief; follow up; improve the programme; feed leadership; share good practice (ch10).
Trade-offs: audits without independence, competence, or verifiable evidence breach Annex H principles; reports that assert levels without working papers fail the next cycle (ch10).

## Assurance Self-Assessment Toolkit scoring
When: a Defence Area chooses Annex G to run structured 1LOD self-assessment against the 12 Elements (ch11).
How: answer Expectation questions per Element sheet with Unsatisfactory/Limited/Moderate/Substantial/N/A; record evidence, non-conformances or observations, and actions; let Score Summary convert 1-4 point ratings to Element and overall percentages (N/A excluded); use document lists and Non-Conformance Summary as aides; archive the completed toolkit with evidence (ch11).
Trade-offs: toolkit use is not mandated by DDSS; a green score without retained evidence is not assurance; N/A must be a scoped reason, not a silent skip (ch11).

## Letters of delegation with safety content
When: appointing people who hold delegated authority for control of activity or safety roles (ch06, ch07).
How: appoint formally by letter; include Annex E safety aspects (risk, assurance, change); ensure training and competence to discharge the role; accept and file the letter (ch06, ch07).
Trade-offs: paper appointments without DSA-approved training, recorded activity lists, or accepted letters fail the supervision and competence Expectations (ch06, ch07).
